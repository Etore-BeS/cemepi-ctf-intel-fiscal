\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Enrolled value-added tax debt is the part of the tax that the administration has established and has not yet collected.
Most of the compliance literature stops earlier.
It asks whether the taxpayer reports the base, and whether third-party information makes concealment harder \citep{allingham1972,kleven2011,pomeranz2015}.
Collection starts after that margin.
Once a debt has been enrolled, the administration has a stock, a list of certificates, and a set of firms.
In S\~ao Paulo that stock is not one object.
ICMS, the state tax on goods and selected services, reaches the attorney office as declared debt or as assessed debt.
Declared debt is tax the firm reported and did not pay.
Assessed debt is tax fixed by an assessment.
Both are enrolled in the \emph{d\'ivida ativa}.
They do not have the same size, the same age, or the same relation between payment and the remaining balance.

The first question is descriptive, and it is aimed at compliance studies.
How do size, concentration, payment and recovery differ between declared and assessed debts?
If the differences are large, a study that pools the enrolled stock will mix a mass of small declared certificates with a much smaller set of large assessed certificates.
A value-weighted moment will then describe a different object from a count-weighted moment.
The same mix matters for the tax administration.
A collection rule that treats every certificate alike will spend effort where the count is, which is not where the stock is.

The December 2025 portfolio of legal entities gives a clear version of that mix.
Assessed debt is 59,542 certificates and \brl{}311.40 billion.
Declared debt is 2,257,189 certificates and \brl{}116.42 billion.
The rank-biserial correlation for certificate values is 0.89, so a random assessed certificate is very likely to exceed a random declared certificate.
The largest 1~percent of certificates in the pooled book, 23,137 certificates, hold 75.8~percent of the stock.
Inside each segment the same cut holds 39.5~percent of declared stock and 39.9~percent of assessed stock.
The pooled concentration is therefore not a property of each segment.
It is produced by assessed debt, which is 72.8~percent of the two-segment stock and 2.6~percent of the certificates.
Of the 23,137 certificates in the pooled top 1~percent, 18,230 are assessed.
Payment and recovery do not rank the segments in the same order.
Some payment is more common on assessed certificates (17.4~percent against 9.1~percent), but payments are 0.48~percent of the assessed stock and 4.73~percent of the declared stock.
The second question is which partial associations of payment and of the balance this cross-section supports.
A linearity and group-heterogeneity diagnostic selects the specification, and the selected models are then fit.

The paper makes two contributions, and a narrower methodological point that is only as strong as the cutoff we document.
Beers, Hatch, Saldana and Wilson follow cohorts of tax liabilities and compare collection by the origin of the liability \citep{beers2015collectibility}.
The IRS Collectibility Curve is concerned mainly with individual income tax in the United States.
This paper is evidence for business VAT portfolios in S\~ao Paulo.
ICMS is that value-added tax.
Although the literature examines the payment of overdue obligations and recognises differences between self-declared debts and debts constituted by assessment, it remains relevant to understand how these origins are associated with the composition and the payment patterns of business VAT portfolios in different institutional settings.
This study offers evidence on the S\~ao Paulo ICMS by jointly examining size, concentration, the occurrence of payments, and amounts paid relative to the remaining balance.
The contribution consists in showing how composition by origin changes the interpretation of the aggregate indicators of the \emph{d\'ivida ativa}, and in providing evidence for research on payment mechanisms and on the design of collection.
The practical contribution is for collection.
The attorney office meets most of the value in a thin assessed tail.
A review of the largest 1~percent of certificates covers 75.8~percent of the pooled stock, and that list is mostly assessed debt.
Declared debt is a different operational problem: many small certificates, usually several on the same firm, with a higher recovery relative to the remaining stock.
We do not estimate the effect of working that list.
The methodological point is limited on purpose.
We do not offer a new estimator.
The partial associations use a logit, restricted cubic splines and quantile regression, in the form the diagnostic selects.
We also show why a round 1~percent cutoff is not arbitrary in this portfolio, and why the same cutoff does not have the same content inside each segment.
The justification is the shape of the Lorenz curve and the fact that the resulting list is a set of certificates the administration can actually review.

The paper proceeds as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:background} sets out the institutional path from the VAT to the enrolled certificate, and locates ICMS against the European VAT only as framing.
Section~\ref{sec:literature} places the question in the compliance and enforcement literature and states descriptive expectations, not hypotheses.
Section~\ref{sec:design} states the hypotheses and links them to the data, the measures and the tests, including the registry-timing caveat and the definition of recovery.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the comparisons, the selected models and the origin estimates.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets them.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
